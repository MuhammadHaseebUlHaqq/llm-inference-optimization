Day 17  ·  Speculative decoding: buying tokens with spare arithmetic

This is a technique that was not run in this repo, which changes how it should be answered. Do not claim a measurement. Instead, derive why it works from the step-time model that was measured, and state the break-even condition. A derived projection with an explicit "I have not run this" is a much better answer than a recited paper summary.

17.1 The observation the whole technique rests on

From Day 13: a decode step costs T_fixed + c1 x batch, with T_fixed around 11 ms and c1 around 0.20 ms per sequence. T_fixed is the shared weight read, 3.09 GB streamed to advance the sequence by one token. The tensor cores are idle through nearly all of it: arithmetic intensity around 1 against a ridge point of 142.

Now ask the key question. If instead of one token, the same forward pass scored six candidate tokens, what would it cost? Almost the same, because the 3.09 GB weight read does not change and the extra arithmetic is free at intensity 1. The GPU has been doing nothing with its arithmetic for the entire step.

The one-sentence version. "Decode is memory-bound, so verifying k drafted tokens in a single forward pass costs about the same as generating one, since you re-use the same weight read. Speculative decoding spends the arithmetic that a memory-bound step was wasting anyway, and buys tokens with it."

17.2 The algorithm, in four steps

  1. Draft. A small, fast model generates k candidate tokens autoregressively.
  2. Verify. The large target model runs one forward pass over the prompt plus all k drafts, producing its own distribution at every one of those positions.
  3. Accept or reject. Walk the drafts left to right. Accept a token with probability min(1, p_target(x) / p_draft(x)). Stop at the first rejection.
  4. Correct. At the rejection point, sample from the residual distribution norm(max(0, p_target - p_draft)). If all k were accepted, sample one bonus token from the target's distribution at position k+1, which the verify pass already computed for free.

So a round produces between 1 and k+1 tokens, and never zero. That floor of 1 is what makes the technique safe: the worst case is a wasted draft, not a stall.

17.3 Why the output distribution is exactly unchanged

This is the property to be able to state precisely, and it is the reason speculative decoding is called lossless rather than approximate.

The accept rule plus the residual-sampling correction together produce samples from p_target exactly. Intuitively: accepting with probability min(1, p_target/p_draft) keeps a token whenever the target likes it at least as much as the draft did; where the draft over-proposed a token, the shortfall is exactly the mass max(0, p_target - p_draft), and sampling the rejection from that renormalised residual puts the mass back where the target wanted it. Summed over both branches, the probability of emitting any given token is p_target. It is the same trick as rejection sampling with a proposal distribution, applied per position.

The consequence to say out loud: the draft model's quality affects speed only, never output quality. A bad draft model means a low acceptance rate and a slow run, not a worse answer. That is why speculative decoding is deployable in a way that, say, aggressive quantization is not.

17.5 Why the win is capped, and where it disappears

  • It is a latency technique, not a throughput technique. It works by spending idle arithmetic. At batch 64 that arithmetic is no longer idle: from Day 13, the step is already at its skinny-GEMM ceiling and c1 x batch is 54% of the step. Speculation and batching compete for the same resource, and batching usually wins on a loaded server.
  • It costs KV memory. The drafts occupy KV blocks during verification, and rejected ones must be rolled back. On a card that OOMs at 123,565 tokens, that is not free.
  • Acceptance rate is workload-dependent. Code and structured text draft well because they are predictable; open-ended creative text drafts badly. A number measured on one workload does not transfer.

17.7 Self-test, Day 17

Q1. Why does verifying five tokens cost about the same as generating one?
Because batch-1 decode is memory-bound at an arithmetic intensity near 1 against a ridge of 142. The cost is the 3.09 GB weight read, which a single forward pass pays once no matter how many query positions it scores. On my measurements that read is T_fixed, about 11 ms of an 11.28 ms step, and the extra positions cost roughly c1 = 0.20 ms each.

Q2. Does speculative decoding change the model's output?
No, and that is the point of the accept-reject rule. Accept with probability min(1, p_target/p_draft), and on rejection sample from the normalised residual max(0, p_target - p_draft). The two branches sum to exactly p_target. The draft model's quality determines speed, never quality.

Q3. When would you not use it?
When acceptance is low or the server is loaded. On my step-time constants, k=5 with a 0.5 acceptance rate is 25% slower than plain decoding, because the round costs 29.7 ms and yields 1.97 tokens against a break-even of 2.63. And at high batch the idle arithmetic it exploits is already spoken for: at batch 64 more than half my step is per-sequence work, not the shared weight read.

18.1 Tensor parallelism: the split, and the two all-reduces

Split each layer's weights across GPUs so every device holds a slice and they cooperate on one forward pass. Megatron's arrangement, which everything since copies:

ATTENTION
  split by HEAD. each GPU gets a subset of the query heads and their
  matching KV heads, computes attention for those heads only, and holds
  the corresponding COLUMN slice of o_proj.
  -> one all-reduce after o_proj to sum the partial outputs.

MLP
  gate_proj and up_proj are split COLUMN-wise (each GPU gets a slice of
  the 8960-wide intermediate). down_proj is split ROW-wise to match.
  -> one all-reduce after down_proj.

TOTAL: 2 all-reduces per transformer layer, per token.
For this model: 28 layers x 2 = 56 collectives per decode step.
The model-specific constraint that makes this concrete. Qwen2.5-1.5B has 12 query heads and 2 KV heads under GQA. Tensor parallelism splits by head, so the KV heads cap the degree: TP=2 gives each GPU 6 query heads and 1 KV head, which is clean. TP=4 cannot split 2 KV heads four ways, so it has to replicate them, which means every GPU stores the full KV cache for its replicated head and the memory saving TP was supposed to give partly evaporates. GQA and tensor parallelism interact, and this is the sentence that shows it.

18.2 Why it wants NVLink, in bytes and in microseconds

Example 18-A: the collective cost at batch 1, TP=2 
hidden = 1536, fp16. one all-reduce moves one hidden vector per token:
  1536 x 2 bytes = 3072 B = 3 KiB

per decode step: 56 all-reduces x 3 KiB = 168 KiB of traffic per token.

BYTES: 168 KiB over PCIe 4.0 x16 (~25 GB/s) = 6.9 us. nothing.
LATENCY: each collective costs a fixed launch-plus-sync of roughly
  8 us over PCIe, or roughly 2 us over NVLink.
    PCIe:   56 x 8 us = 0.45 ms per token
    NVLink: 56 x 2 us = 0.11 ms per token

against a TP=2 step of about 5.5 ms (T_fixed halved), that is
  7.6% overhead on PCIe, 2.0% on NVLink.

The conclusion is the interesting part: decode-time tensor parallelism is latency-bound on the collective, not bandwidth-bound. The payload is 3 KiB. What hurts is paying a synchronisation 56 times per token, on a step that is only a few milliseconds. That is why NVLink matters for TP and why TP degree does not scale gracefully over PCIe. Prefill is the opposite case, because the payload scales with sequence length and the bandwidth term starts to matter.

Projected TP=2 speedup on this model: T_fixed halves from 11 ms to about 5.5 ms, so the step goes 11.28 to roughly 5.95 ms including the PCIe collective cost. That is 1.9x, not 2x, and the missing 0.1x is the 56 synchronisations.

18.3 Pipeline parallelism, one sentence

Split the model by layer rather than within a layer, so GPU 0 holds layers 0 to 13 and GPU 1 holds 14 to 27. It is a throughput technique, not a latency technique, because a single token still traverses every stage in sequence and the pipeline has bubbles at the start and end of every batch. Use it when you need to fit a model across nodes; use TP when you need the latency.

20.1 Anchor 1: the FlashAttention derivation

The only anchor with no supporting number in this repo, so the derivation has to carry it.

BEAT 1  standard attention writes the full N x N score matrix to HBM, reads
        it back for softmax, writes it, reads it again for the V multiply.
        the FLOPs are unavoidable. the round trips are not.

BEAT 2  the obstacle: softmax needs the whole row for its denominator, which
        looks incompatible with tiling.

BEAT 3  online softmax fixes it. carry m, l, O. when a tile arrives with a
        bigger max, rescale by c = exp(m_old - m_new) and continue. exact,
        not approximate. it is the same max-subtraction you already need for
        numerical stability, turned into an incremental algorithm.

BEAT 4  so FlashAttention tiles Q, K, V into SRAM and never materialises the
        score matrix. HBM traffic falls from O(N^2) to O(N); compute is
        unchanged, and the backward pass does MORE arithmetic by recomputing.
        it is an IO-complexity result.

BEAT 5  the inference caveat, which is what shows you thought about decode:
        at batch 1 the score matrix is 1 x n. no quadratic term to avoid, no
        tile worth keeping. decode's problem is the opposite, not enough
        parallel work. the answer is flash-decoding / split-KV, which splits
        the KV sequence across SMs and merges partials with the same rescale.
        in my own vLLM trace: 28 split-KV kernels and 28 combine kernels per
        step, one pair per layer, at 14.9 to 16.2 us each.

20.2 Anchor 2: the KV-cache OOM

SETUP   I wanted to watch the KV cache kill a GPU rather than compute that it
        would. Qwen2.5-1.5B fp16 on a 12GB RTX 3060, 32-token prompt, context
        grown purely by decoding one token at a time, VRAM logged every 1000.
        six hours of sequential decode. it died at 123,565 tokens.

MATH    analytical: 2 x 28 layers x 2 KV heads x 128 head_dim x 2 bytes
                  = 28,672 B = 28 KiB/token.
        the 2 KV heads size the cache, not the 12 query heads. that is GQA.

GAP     measured slope was 62 KiB/token, 2.21x the prediction, and sat above
        it the whole way. that gap is a MECHANISM, not overhead:
        HuggingFace reallocates the whole cache every step. torch.cat cannot
        grow a tensor in place, so it allocates a fresh block for n+1 tokens
        and copies n in, and both copies are live during the copy. peak is
        weights + 2 x KV. at 120k that predicts 9507 MiB; I measured 10,227,
        the ~700 MiB residual being attention scratch that also grows with n.

CRASH   and the death is FRAGMENTATION, not exhaustion. at OOM: 10,437 MiB
        allocated, 11,764 MiB reserved. 1.3 GiB free inside PyTorch's own
        pool and unusable, because the next cat needed one contiguous 2 x KV
        slab and the free space was scattered between parked blocks. enough
        empty parking spaces for the bus, no unbroken stretch to fit it.

OWN IT  I predicted the card would die near 30k tokens and kept the wrong
        guess in the writeup. it reached 123k. the mechanism I named was
        right and my arithmetic was pessimistic.

LAND    both mechanisms are exactly what PagedAttention removes: fixed-size
        blocks with a block table, so there is no cat, no second copy, and
        any freed block fits any future need. that is the paper's "external
        fragmentation" category, and I watched it before I read the name.

20.3 Anchor 3: the batching knee

WHY     batch 1 wastes the GPU: the step reads all 3.09 GB of weights to
        advance one sequence by one token. intensity ~1, tensor cores idle.
        so I swept vLLM concurrency 1 to 256 on the same 512-token prompt.

WHAT    decode throughput 88.6 -> 2696 tok/s from batch 1 to 64, a 30x gain,
        then essentially stopped: 2967 at batch 256, while TPOT climbed
        11.28 -> 86.29 ms. the knee is 64.

MODEL   step = T_fixed + c1 x batch. T_fixed ~ 11 ms is the shared weight
        read paid once regardless of batch; c1 ~ 0.20 ms is what each added
        sequence costs. throughput = batch / (T_fixed + c1 x batch): linear
        while the fixed cost dominates, asymptotic to 1/c1 ~ 3000 once it
        does not. knee where they cross: ~58 predicted, 64 measured.

TWIST   the plateau is not a roofline. at batch 256 I am at ~9 TFLOPS (16%
        of ~51) and ~90 GB/s (25% of 360). neither is saturated. the limit
        is operation shape: batched decode is a skinny GEMM with M = batch,
        occupancy-bound, and the paged KV reads are scattered. the naive
        roofline predicts an ideal batch of 142 against a measured 64, and
        that 2x-4x gap is itself the result.

TEST    I made it falsifiable. I predicted the knee is a model property, not
        a card property, because it is the weight-read-versus-KV-read
        crossover. on an RTX 3090, 2.6x bandwidth and 1.4x compute, every
        throughput number scaled 2.4x-3.4x and the knee stayed at 64, TPOT
        flat through 64 and breaking at 96 on both cards. I got the plateau
        HEIGHT wrong: predicted 6-7k from bandwidth alone, measured 10,100,
        because the 3090's extra compute also lifts the skinny-GEMM ceiling.

20.4 Anchor 4: the HF-versus-vLLM profiling result

CLAIM   vLLM decodes ~3.2x faster than HuggingFace at batch 1, same card,
        same model. the usual explanation is "CUDA graphs and fused kernels",
        which is an argument, not a measurement. so I profiled it as a 2x2
        ablation over torch.compile and CUDA-graph capture, plus HF.

HEAD    of the 28.09 ms per token separating them, 25.34 ms (90%) is device
        idle removed and 2.60 ms (9%) is faster device work. the gap is a
        SCHEDULING problem, and now there is a number behind that sentence.
        HF fires 1198 kernels with 1198 CPU launches and is 62.3% idle.
        per-launch idle is only 21.1 us against a 12.8 us average kernel: it
        is not that a launch is expensive, it is that it is paid 1198 times,
        and batch-1 decode kernels are microsecond matrix-vector products,
        so the dispatch chain above a kernel costs more than the kernel.

SPLIT   the ablation separated two things the usual story conflates.
        FUSION is vLLM's hand-written kernels, before any compiler runs:
        with compile and graphs off on both sides, kernel count falls
        1198 -> 356, a 3.4x cut. fused RMSNorm, fused rotary, fused
        SiLU-and-multiply, paged FlashAttention.
        CUDA GRAPHS change no kernels at all: counts are identical with
        graphs on and off, but CPU launches fall 356 -> 17 and idle goes to
        zero. I stated the prediction first: if graphs remove idle and
        nothing else, the graphs-on step should equal the graphs-off busy
        time. predicted 13.02 and 12.90 ms, measured 12.57 and 12.56.
        within 3.5% and 2.6%.

BEST    the result I did not predict is the most useful: the two are NOT
        additive. torch.compile saves 2.81 ms with graphs off and 0.01 ms
        with graphs on. once graphs take the CPU off the critical path,
        making the CPU's per-launch work cheaper is worth nothing. measuring
        only the shipping default against plain eager would have credited
        graphs with a fusion win, or the reverse, with no way to tell.

NEXT    and it closes the question of what is left. against this card's
        MEASURED achievable read bandwidth of 291.5 GB/s, not the 360 GB/s
        spec figure, vLLM's decode step runs at 85%. there is almost nothing
        left to win from scheduling at batch 1. the next lever has to be
        arithmetic intensity, which is the batching experiment.

Naming these is a positive signal. "Aware of it, have not gone deep" is a correct and well-calibrated answer. Hedging or bluffing is not.

CutThe reason, in the form to say it
MoE routing and expert parallelism"Single-GPU dense-model artifact. Everything I measured is about weight-read amortisation, which MoE changes qualitatively. I would rather be precise about dense inference than vague about both."
Ring attention, sequence parallelism"Multi-GPU long-context technique. I have one card, and my long-context result is a memory result, not a distribution result."
FP8 and FlashAttention-3"Hopper-class features. My card is Ampere sm_86. I could recite them but not measure them, and reciting is the failure mode I am avoiding."
MLA beyond one sentence"One sentence is enough: it compresses K and V into a shared low-rank latent, so it attacks the same KV-bytes term as GQA but harder. I can place it on the MHA-MQA-GQA axis; I have not derived it."
P/D disaggregation (DistServe, Mooncake)"Multi-node serving architecture. I understand the motivation directly from my own data, since prefill is compute-bound at 4205 tok/s and decode is memory-bound at 28, so co-locating them means one phase always interferes with the other. I have not studied the systems."
Structured decoding, multi-LoRA"Serving features orthogonal to the performance question this artifact is about."
Training-side anything: ZeRO, FSDP, activation checkpointing"Out of scope by construction. Inference has no backward pass, no optimizer state and no gradient memory, which is exactly why the KV cache is the dominant growing tenant rather than activations."
Writing a production CUDA kernel"Three weeks buys diagnosis, not authorship. I can read a kernel and tell you why it is slow in coalescing, occupancy and shared-memory terms. Claiming authorship I cannot demonstrate is worse than claiming neither."

21.3 The habit that decides whether any of this worked

End every topic by explaining it out loud, unaided, in under 90 seconds. If you cannot, re-derive it from scratch rather than rereading. Rereading produces recognition; re-derivation produces defence, and only one of those survives being interrupted with "wait, why is it two KV heads and not twelve?"

The measure of a day is not what you read. It is what you can now say cold.

21.4 Final checklist, all three weeks

  1. Whiteboard attention shapes through one decode step of this config, unaided.
  2. State the ridge point (142 FLOP/byte), where decode sits (intensity ~1), and where prefill sits.
  3. Hand over two versions of a matmul kernel and explain which is faster, in coalescing, shared-versus-global and occupancy terms, with numbers.
  4. Derive the online-softmax rescale on a blank page and verify it against a direct computation.
  5. Say why FlashAttention does nothing at batch-1 decode, and what does instead.
  6. Tell the OOM story in under 90 seconds with four numbers in it.
  7. Name both batching plateaus, their values, and the distinct mechanism behind each.
  8. Give the idle-versus-kernel split of the vLLM win, and why the two optimizations are not additive.
  9. Explain why your 4-bit run got slower, and name the two assumptions that would have to change.
  10. Deliver all four anchor answers cold, timed, and interrupted.
The last thing to remember. The strongest material in this repo is not the speedups. It is the three places where a prediction failed and was diagnosed: the OOM cliff came at 123k tokens rather than the 30k predicted, the 3090 plateau came in at 10,100 tok/s rather than the 6-7k predicted, and the 4-bit run got slower rather than faster. Lead with those when asked what you learned. Anyone can report a number that confirmed what they expected.
